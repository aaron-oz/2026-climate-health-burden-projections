Answers to the four questions in Aaron's notes of the 2026-11-09 team meeting
(docs/meetings/team-meeting-2026-11-09-notes.org). Each says what was checked
and how. Numbers are from the pooled output of 2026-11-06 (24 common climate

* 1. What future populations do we use, are they fixed by GBD, do they vary by scenario, and where do the assumptions come from?

Two populations enter, in different roles.

- National population by age and sex. Source:
  =~/data/wb-temp-attr-projections/gbd-forecasts/population.csv= (6.9 GB,
  file dated 2026-06-02, IHME's GBD 2021 forecast download that came with the
  cause forecasts; 500 draws per location, age group, sex and year,
  2021-2050).
  It does two jobs:
  - It turns IHME's forecast death rates into death counts. For each location,
    cause, age, sex and year, deaths = mean over draws of (rate draw x
    population draw) (global-scripts/util_convert_ihme_forecast.R, header).
  - It is the denominator of every rate in the report: the mean over the 500
    draws, averaged over a decade's years
    (output/report-multimodel-2026-11/build_tables.R, which reads it via
    pop_by_loc.rds).
  It is the same in all four climate scenarios. The scenarios differ only in
  temperature; IHME's population and mortality forecasts are held fixed
  (Workflow B scales attributable deaths by scenario, it does not touch the
  population). So yes: fixed by GBD, and not varying by scenario.
- Gridded population within each country. Source: Gridded Population of the
  World v4 rev 11 (GPW), as served by the World Bank Climate Change Knowledge
  Portal at 0.25 degrees, in 20-year averages (2020-2039, 2040-2059), one file
  per SSP (global-scripts/util_run_cckp_pipeline.R, POP_DATASET and
  year_to_pop_bucket). It only weights pixels within a country: pixel weight
  = pixel population / the country's gridded total, so only where people
  live relative to the temperature matters, never the totals. This one does
  differ by scenario: each scenario's run spreads the same IHME population
  over the country according to that SSP's spatial pattern. How much the
  weights differ, and where the files come from:
  docs/reviews/gridded-pop-by-scenario-2026-11-13.org (median location: under
  2% of people placed differently; a few countries, led by Tajikistan and
  Kyrgyzstan, differ more).

Where the national assumptions come from: IHME's GBD 2021 forecasting work,
whose reference scenario IHME describes as roughly SSP2-4.5 (our methodology
walkthrough, docs/methodology/methodology-walkthrough.tex, "The unscale/rescale
problem"). Citation still to be verified before it goes into a document: the
population forecast is published in the GBD 2021 fertility and forecasting
papers (Lancet, 2024) and its method descends from Vollset et al. (Lancet,
2020). We have not read IHME's own download metadata to confirm which release
the file is; the file itself does not say.

* 2. Why do low-income countries have lower rates than lower-middle-income countries?

(Aaron, 2026-11-13: the question is low income lower than lower-middle income.)

Crude rates, SSP2-4.5, deaths per 100,000 people per year:

| Decade    | Heat, low | Heat, lower middle | Cold, low | Cold, lower middle |
|-----------+-----------+--------------------+-----------+--------------------|
| 2022-2030 |      4.55 |               7.97 |      5.66 |               6.75 |
| 2041-2050 |      5.16 |              10.74 |      5.41 |               7.48 |

Mostly age structure, as far as our three age groups can show.

- People aged 65 and over are 4.8% of the population in low-income countries
  in 2041-2050, against 10.6% in lower-middle income (3.2% and 6.3% in
  2022-2030), and 65+ is where most temperature deaths are (48% of heat and 80%
  of cold deaths in low income, 67% and 82% in lower middle, 2041-2050).
- A standard split of the gap (Kitagawa: average of the two one-sided
  counterfactuals), done on the three age groups we hold (under 5, 15-49, 65+;
  together 78 to 83% of each group's deaths):
  - Heat: age structure accounts for 64% of the gap in 2022-2030 and 71% in
    2041-2050. The rest is that heat death rates within age are lower in low
    income: at 65+, 50.9 against 67.7 per 100,000 in 2041-2050 (under 5 is the
    other way: 3.5 against 2.3).
  - Cold: age structure accounts for more than the whole gap (258% in
    2041-2050). At the same age structure, low-income countries would have the
    higher cold rate: at 65+, 89.9 against 57.4 per 100,000 in 2041-2050.
- Limits: the split ignores ages 5-14 and 50-64 (no deaths by those ages in
  the export), uses means without uncertainty, and is on the 2026-11-06
  pooled output. Age-standardized rates from the new summary run will answer
  this properly. Why low-income heat rates within age are lower (climate,
  cause mix, IHME's baseline death rates) is not examined.

* 3. Do we have age-structure tables for projections? If not, is that a strong vote for age-standardized rates?

- Population: yes. population.csv has every 5-year age group (Under 5, 5 to
  9, ..., 95 plus) by sex for every location and year 2021-2050, which is
  what an age-standardized rate needs on the population side.
- Deaths: not here. The per-draw export we hold has deaths in three age
  groups only (under 5, 15-49, 65+, by sex). The Workflow B files on the
  production machine have every age group (0-4, 5-9, ..., 75-79, 80+), cause,
  sex and draw, so the age detail exists; it was not exported.
- So the age structure exists on both sides; what is missing is the export.
  Aaron's ruling (2026-11-13): age-standardized rates are computed on the
  production machine within the new summary run, not from a data dump.
  Crude and age-standardized rates can both be reported. Standard population:
  GBD world standard or WHO 2000-2025, Aaron asking Charlie; both will be
  computed. Our age groups end at 80+, so a standard's older groups are
  summed into 80+.

* 4. How much work is it for Caspar to make age-sex-cause splits by decade and scenario?

- Machine time: the last export step read all Workflow B files once and was
  benchmarked on the production machine at about 153 CPU-hours, about 10
  hours on 16 workers (34 GB memory) or 5 hours on 32 (reported 2026-10-27,
  3 sample countries; about 52 GB of input per country, 51 to 53 seconds per
  GB). A new run has to read the same files again, so expect at least that.
  The extra cost of the finer split and of pooling on that machine is not
  measured yet; it will be measured on the test fixture
  (output/review-workflow-b-2026-10-26/test_draw_export/) before the request
  goes out.
- His time: one command to start, if the new code works as delivered, plus
  sending back summary tables (size to be estimated with the code).
- Our time: writing and testing that code is the larger part. Its scope
  (whether the joint age x sex x cause split is wanted for every country or
  only for income groups, regions and the world) sets both the code's size
  and the run's memory; put to Aaron as a decision on 2026-11-13.
